What is this platform?

The GeoAI Urban Mapping Platform is an interactive geospatial framework that turns multi-source geospatial data into detailed urban information. It analyses buildings, road networks, urban services and environmental indicators to classify built-up intensity, monitor urban patterns and generate decision-ready maps and statistics.

Key features

  • Automated multi-source data integration
  • 30 urban and spatial indicators
  • Built-up intensity classification (low, medium, high)
  • Interactive web-based mapping
  • Statistics and analytical reports
  • Spatiotemporal change analysis
  • Scalable and applicable to any city or region

Applications & benefits

  • Informal settlement analysis

    Methodology

    Every analysis follows the same fifteen-step workflow, from the boundary you draw to the indicators a decision-maker reads. Each step below is implemented in the web platform; the Python pipeline in the repository runs the same steps at regional scale.

    1. Area of interest (AOI)

      The user defines the study area directly on the interactive map. The AOI becomes the spatial boundary for data acquisition, processing, analysis and prediction. Using the actual polygon rather than only its bounding box prevents areas outside the study boundary from biasing area, density and coverage calculations.

    2. Multi-source data acquisition

      The platform integrates Sentinel-2 imagery, high-resolution imagery when available, OpenStreetMap, Overture Maps building footprints, road networks and supporting layers. Multi-source integration reduces dependence on a single dataset and improves completeness across different locations. In the browser, OpenStreetMap is queried live through the Overpass API, including historical snapshots.

    3. Satellite image selection & mosaicking

      Sentinel-2 L2A scenes are selected by time period, spatial coverage and cloud cover from the Earth Search STAC catalogue. When the AOI spans more than one scene, the least cloudy valid pixel is taken at each location, giving continuous coverage for consistent indicators and temporal analysis.

    4. Geospatial data pre-processing

      Inputs are clipped to the AOI, coordinates are harmonised, clouds and shadows are masked using the scene classification layer, and geometries are validated: rings are closed, repeated vertices removed and self-intersecting footprints repaired. This stops inconsistencies in the source data from propagating into the GeoAI analysis.

    5. Building data fusion & deduplication

      Footprints from multiple sources are fused, and spatial and geometric relationships are used to identify duplicates: two footprints match when their intersection over union is at least 0.5, or when one sits inside the other and covers most of it. Deduplication happens before feature extraction because duplicates inflate building counts, density, built-up area and coverage.

    6. Road network processing

      Roads within the AOI are processed into road length, road density, junctions, the share of major roads and distance to the nearest major road. Roads complement building morphology by describing accessibility and the structure of the urban fabric.

    7. Spectral analysis

      Spectral indices such as NDVI and NDBI are derived from the red, near-infrared and short-wave infrared bands. Building vectors describe urban geometry, while spectral information adds evidence about vegetation, built-up surfaces and land cover.

    8. Spatial grid generation

      The AOI is divided into regular 250 × 250 m analysis cells aligned to a fixed origin, so the same place always falls in the same cell across runs and dates. A consistent grid gives comparable spatial units while balancing local detail and computational efficiency. The cell size can be changed to 100 m or 500 m.

    9. Feature engineering

      Thirty urban and geospatial indicators are calculated for every cell, covering building morphology, density, coverage, roads, spatial distribution, services, vegetation, water and spectral response. They turn heterogeneous GIS and remote-sensing data into structured machine-learning features.

    10. Ground truth & training data

      Representative cells receive reference labels of low, medium or high built-up intensity. Initial labels are generated automatically from coverage, building density, floor area ratio and road density, keeping only the clearest cells. They are a starting point, not final ground truth: users review them on the map and can relabel any cell before retraining.

    11. Random Forest training & validation

      A Random Forest of 100 trees is trained on the engineered indicators, with a stratified 70/30 split held back for validation. Performance is reported with a confusion matrix, overall accuracy, Cohen's kappa, and precision, recall and F1-score per class. Random Forest suits structured geospatial variables, non-linear relationships and feature interactions.

    12. GeoAI spatial prediction

      The trained model is applied to every analysis cell to predict low, medium or high built-up intensity. The share of trees voting for the winning class is reported as a confidence value, turning machine-learning output into spatially explicit urban intelligence.

    13. Change detection

      The same grid and model are applied to an earlier snapshot of the data. Cells are marked as intensified, declined, stable or newly developed, alongside changes in building count, built-up area and road length.

    14. Interactive mapping & export

      Results are explored as map layers: intensity, confidence, any of the 30 indicators, change and training labels. Everything can be exported as CSV and GeoJSON for ArcGIS and QGIS, and as a PDF report.

    15. Decision support

      The platform summarises where intensity is highest, where dense areas are far from health or education services, where growth is happening and where the source data looks incomplete, so priorities rest on evidence rather than assumption.

    Limits worth knowing

    • Results are only as complete as the source data. Where OpenStreetMap is sparse, add footprints from another source; cells with roads but no buildings are flagged.
    • Automatic seed labels come from four of the indicators, so validation scores measure agreement with those rules until you add manual labels.
    • Building levels are often missing in OpenStreetMap; a single storey is assumed where none is recorded, which understates floor area ratio in tall districts.
    • Historical snapshots show what was mapped at that date, not necessarily what was built, so early dates can overstate growth.
